Section 1

The pitch — three lengths, memorise all three

The one sentence

We predict how flood-prone a piece of land will be after it is built on — not just how it looks today — and we prove it against places in Kochi that were empty before the 2018 floods and have been built on since.

The 30 seconds

Every flood susceptibility map in our field is built the same way: take the land as it looks now, take where flooding was seen before, and train a model to connect the two. That answers “which existing neighbourhoods flood?” It does not answer the question a family or a planner actually has: “if I build here, what happens?” When you build, the plot stops absorbing water and starts shedding it, and the water it sheds lands on the plots downhill. We add three things to a standard machine-learning flood model so it can answer that: a feature that follows water downhill to measure how much paved surface drains into each spot (UICA), a second prediction for each plot “as if built”, and an honest test using real development that already happened.

The paragraph (for the viva)

Say this verbatim if asked “summarise your project”

“We build a flood-susceptibility mapping framework for the Kochi / Periyar basin that answers a question existing maps cannot: what happens to a plot of land once it is built on. Existing models — including the published Kerala susceptibility map — read land cover as it stands today, treat every pixel as independent, assume anywhere flooding was not observed is safe, and validate on randomly split data that leaks neighbouring pixels between training and testing. We change all four. We route sealed surface downhill to compute how much runoff drains into each cell (UICA) and how that has grown with new construction (ΔUICA); we train under a positive-unlabeled framing that never assumes an unflooded cell is safe; we evaluate with spatial block cross-validation and a temporal hold-out across the 2018, 2019 and 2021 events, reporting the inflated conventional number alongside so the difference is visible; and we predict two susceptibility values per cell — as it is, and as it would be once developed — validating the difference against plots that were empty before 2018 and built on since. All data is free and public, and three of our four reference papers name this direction as their own stated future work.”

Why the panel should care

Kerala lost 400+ lives and evacuated 1.2 million people in and after the 2018 floods. Kochi keeps building on low-lying land in the Periyar basin. A map that only shows past flooding tells that construction nothing. Ours is meant to be consulted before the concrete is poured.

Section 2

Vocabulary you must get right

Two members using different words for the same thing is what makes a panel suspicious. Lock these down.

The words that get confused

TermWhat we mean by itDo not say…
Flood susceptibilityThe relative tendency of a place to flood, based on its terrain and land-cover conditions. A standing map, not tied to any one storm. This is our word — use it.“flood prediction” / “flood forecast” — that implies we predict a specific future event with a probability. We do not.
Flood hazardThe physical flood itself — depth, extent, how often. We do not model this directly.using it interchangeably with susceptibility
Flood vulnerabilityStrictly: exposure + sensitivity + coping capacity of people and assets. We model only the terrain / land-cover side and say so. The deck title uses “susceptibility” — keep to that.claiming we do a full vulnerability assessment of people
Flood riskHazard × exposure × vulnerability, usually with people and money attached. Bigger than our scope.“we compute flood risk”
Development-aware / prospectiveThe model can answer for land as it would be once built on, not only as it is now. This is the core novelty.—

The acronyms — one line each

TermPlain meaning
FSMFlood Susceptibility Mapping — the general research area.
SARSynthetic Aperture Radar. Sentinel-1 is a radar satellite. Radar sees through cloud and works at night — vital for monsoon Kerala. Good at spotting standing water.
Sentinel-1 / -2Free ESA satellites. S-1 = radar (flood water, cloud-proof). S-2 = optical/colour + near-infrared (land cover, buildings, vegetation). We use both.
DEMDigital Elevation Model — a grid of ground heights. Tells us what is low-lying.
FABDEMA 30 m DEM with tree canopy and buildings machine-learned out, leaving bare earth. We use it instead of SRTM because coconut canopy over low land in Kochi corrupts ordinary DEMs.
CHIRPSA free 40-year daily rainfall dataset (~5 km). We use it as a long-run statistic (how many days per year exceed 10 mm), not as a live storm input.
Otsu / Edge-OtsuA standard automatic method to split a radar image into “water” and “not water” by picking the brightness cut-off. Builds our flood inventory.
Flood inventoryThe map of where flooding actually happened, assembled from past events. It is the “answer key” the model learns from.
TWITopographic Wetness Index — a terrain formula for how much water tends to accumulate at a spot (flat + lots draining in = high).
HANDHeight Above Nearest Drainage — how high a cell sits above the stream it drains to. Low HAND = close to flooding. Strong predictor; none of our four papers uses it.
Flow accumulationFollowing the downhill path of every cell and counting how many cells drain through each point. The basis of UICA.
UICAUpslope Impervious Contributing Area. Flow accumulation, but each upstream cell is weighted by how paved/sealed it is. In words: “how much paved surface sends its water down to this spot.” Our feature — nobody else routes imperviousness downhill.
ΔUICAThe change in UICA between two years — the extra runoff loaded onto a cell purely because new construction appeared uphill.
Impervious surfacePaved or roofed ground that water cannot soak into — roads, roofs, yards, walls.
PU learningPositive-Unlabeled learning. We know where it did flood (positive). Everywhere else is “unlabeled”, not “safe” — radar misses flooding under trees and in cities, and satellites only pass every few days. PU learning handles this instead of pretending unflooded = safe.
Spatial block CVCross-validation where the test area is a whole sub-watershed kept geographically apart from training. Stops neighbouring pixels leaking between train and test.
Temporal hold-outTrain on the 2018 event, test on 2019 and 2021. Checks whether a model still works years after it was fitted.
Leak-free evaluationOur umbrella term for spatial block CV + temporal hold-out, reported next to the ordinary random-split number so the inflation is visible.
LightGBM / XGBoostGradient-boosted decision-tree models. Fast, strong on tabular data, run on a laptop CPU. Our susceptibility model. Also what the Kerala paper found beat expert weighting.
SHAPA method that says how much each factor pushed a given prediction up or down — the “why is this score high” breakdown.
CounterfactualA “what would have to change” statement. Ours only moves things a builder can actually change (paved fraction, setback) and freezes what nobody can (elevation, slope).
DFODartmouth Flood Observatory — a public archive of flood-extent maps. We use its 2018 Kerala polygon as one corroboration source (it is coarse, ~250 m, so not the only one).
Copernicus EMSThe EU’s emergency rapid-mapping service; it produced flood maps for the August 2018 Kerala activation. Second corroboration source.
S_current / S_developed / ΔThe two predictions per cell — susceptibility as land is now, and as it would be if built on — and their difference Δ (the “uplift”). Δ is what a naive before-you-build check silently omits.

05Motivation

On the slide

Urban growth accelerates flood risk faster than climate change alone — every paved acre sheds water instead of absorbing it. Kochi / Periyar basin: dense monsoon-driven urbanisation on low terrain — a high-stakes test case. FSM with satellite + ML is the worldwide standard. But existing FSM answers “where has it flooded before”, not “what happens to this land once it is built on.”

Say it like this

“The last line is our whole motivation. A standard map looks at a green plot and says ‘low risk’. Build a house, a wall and a paved yard on it and the plot now sheds water — but the map still says what it said about the empty plot. The family will live on the built plot, not the empty one.”

If they ask “is the ‘faster than climate change’ claim yours?”

Soften to defensible ground: “In rapidly urbanising basins, land-use change is a large and often dominant driver of increased local flood response between events. We are not ranking it against climate change globally — the point is that it is big, local, and currently unmodelled in these maps.”

07Problem Statement — the gap, in words

On the slide

Existing models assess risk from the current state of the land. They do not adequately capture: (1) future development of currently undeveloped land, (2) upstream → downstream effects of new impervious surfaces, (3) reliable spatial and temporal validation of susceptibility predictions.

Say it like this

“Three concrete gaps. One: the model reads the plot as it is now, so its answer describes a place that will not exist once you build. Two: every model treats each 10 m cell as independent — but if ten plots uphill are paved, more water arrives at your gate even though nothing changed on your land, and no model in our set can represent that. Three: these maps are usually validated on a random split, which lets a pixel’s own neighbours end up in the test set and inflates the score.”

If they ask “are these gaps real or invented?”

“Gaps two and three are named by the authors themselves — Waleed & Sajjad on land-use change, the Kerala paper on cross-validation. Gap one follows directly from how all four build their training data.”

Section 4

The base paper — Mohamadiazar et al. 2024

What the paper does

It builds an AI system that reads satellite radar images plus map data (elevation, soil, paved-surface %, rainfall) and, soon after rain, marks which parts of a city are flooded. Tested on Miami-Dade County, Florida. The model is a U-Net — a deep-learning image-segmentation network. Training labels came from Otsu-thresholding Sentinel-1 radar to auto-mark water. Results on the test set: 93.73% accuracy, F1 89.85%, AUC 0.93, and a “Ground Truth Index” of 84.05% — how often predictions matched real reported flood locations (a city complaint database + geotagged tweets).

Why it is our base paper

  • It is the closest published work that fuses satellite imagery + elevation + a learning model for urban flood prediction, with strong, well-validated numbers.
  • Its own limitations and future-work section reads like our brief: it flags rainfall-to-image timing uncertainty (we sidestep this entirely by not doing event-time prediction), recommends moving preprocessing to Google Earth Engine (we do), and recommends continuously feeding in new imagery to keep the tool “live” (our multi-epoch design turns that suggestion into a measurable test).
  • It gives us a concrete, reproducible baseline and evaluation vocabulary (accuracy / precision / recall / F1 / a ground-truth index) that we extend rather than replace.

What we keep, change, add, drop

MoveDetail
KeepStacking multiple aligned raster layers as model input; the SAR → dB → Edge-Otsu chain to build a flood inventory; standard segmentation and classification metrics for comparability.
ChangeThe core predictor: their U-Net water-segmentation model → our LightGBM/XGBoost susceptibility model on a table of terrain + land-cover + flow features. Their SRTM-type elevation → FABDEM (canopy and buildings removed). Their random 70/30 split → spatial block CV + temporal hold-out.
AddMulti-year design (2017–2024); UICA / ΔUICA flow-routed upslope features; dual prediction (S_current, S_developed, Δ); positive-unlabeled labelling; natural-experiment validation; SHAP + constrained counterfactual advice.
DropLive/same-day rainfall as a required input and near-real-time operation — those belong to their during-storm use case. We use rainfall only as a long-run frequency (CHIRPS, days > 10 mm, 2010–2024).

The sharp question: “You’re not even using their model — how is it your base paper?”

Answer: “A base paper is the work we study closely, reproduce as a baseline, and extend — not a method we copy line for line. We reproduce its flood-inventory pipeline and its evaluation approach, we use it as our comparison baseline, and our whole direction is its own stated future work: keep feeding multi-year imagery in, move to Earth Engine, and get away from the rainfall-timing problem. We changed the final predictor because our question is different — a standing susceptibility map, not a during-storm water mask — and gradient boosting on engineered hydrological features is the right tool for that and is what the closest Kerala study also found strongest.”

If a slide/earlier draft mentioned DeepLabV3+ or a rule-based risk score

An earlier version of our plan used a DeepLabV3+ segmentation model and a hand-weighted risk score. We moved on from the hand-weighted score because it was already published for Kerala and published as the weaker option (Kendagannaswamy: AHP 0.763 vs LightGBM 0.948). A segmentation model (DeepLabV3+ or U-Net) still appears in our pipeline, but only to make the land-cover / built-up change maps in column 3 — the susceptibility model itself is now LightGBM/XGBoost under positive-unlabeled learning. If asked, say exactly that: “segmentation is a pre-processing step for land cover; the susceptibility model is gradient boosting.”

Section 6

The novelty — defended point by point

This is where a panel probes hardest, because “new area + new UI” is not a research contribution and they know it. For each claim: what it is, why it is new against our four papers, and what to say if they push back.

  1. N1 · Prospective, development-aware prediction

    What: two susceptibility values per cell — S_current (land as it is) and S_developed (same cell with land-cover features set to a built state and every downstream cell’s UICA updated) — plus the uplift Δ = S_developed − S_current.

    Why new: all four papers predict only for land as it currently stands. None produces a second, “as-if-built” value.

    If they say “you’re inventing data that doesn’t exist”

    “We are not inventing observations. We take the trained model and evaluate it at a defined built-state feature vector — impervious fraction raised to the observed Kochi residential norm, vegetation reduced accordingly, downstream UICA updated. That is a counterfactual query to the model, and we test whether it is right using land that was actually built on between 2018 and 2021.”

  2. N2 · UICA and ΔUICA

    What: UICA = weighted flow accumulation — for every cell, sum the impervious fraction of every upstream cell that drains into it. ΔUICA = its change over time. One extra raster operation, no extra data.

    Why new: all four use only local imperviousness or vegetation at the cell. None routes it downhill. This is the only feature in our set that lets the model say “your flooding is caused by construction uphill of you.”

    Formula, if asked

    UICA(c) = Σ over all cells u draining into c of [ impervious_fraction(u) × cell_area ] computed with a weighted flow-accumulation routine (WhiteboxTools / pysheds) over a filled FABDEM, D8 or D-infinity flow directions.

  3. N3 · Natural-experiment validation

    What: the treatment group = cells that were unbuilt before the 2018 flood and built by 2021. The control group = cells that stayed unbuilt, matched to the treatment cells on elevation, slope, HAND and road distance by propensity matching. Fit the model on pre-2018 data only; test whether S_developed predicts the treatment cells’ 2021 flood outcome better than S_current does.

    Why new: none of the four validates a susceptibility model against observed development. They validate a static map against a static flood extent.

    The question you should expect: “correlation, not causation”

    Concede it cleanly: “It is a quasi-experiment, not a randomised one. People choose to build on flatter, better-connected land, so treatment and control cells differ systematically. Propensity matching narrows that gap; it does not close it. We will report results as association under matching, never as a proven causal effect. We expect this to be your sharpest question and we would rather state the limit than be caught by it.”

  4. N4 · Leak-free evaluation protocol

    What: three evaluations reported side by side — random split (shown only so the inflation is visible), spatial block CV (folds are whole HydroBASINS level-12 sub-watersheds), temporal hold-out (fit 2018, test 2019 & 2021).

    Why new here: all four use a conventional split. The Kerala paper lists cross-validation as future work. Random splits over raster data leak neighbouring pixels into the test set and inflate AUC by roughly 5–15%, which is why published values cluster at 0.94–0.99.

    Reframe a “your numbers are lower” attack into a finding

    “Under leak-free validation we expect lower numbers than the published 0.948, and that is the finding, not a failure. A defensible 0.85 under spatial block CV is worth more than an inflated 0.95 under a leaky split. Quantifying that gap for the Kerala case is contribution N4.”

  5. N5 · Constrained counterfactual recourse

    What: features are split into mutable (a builder can change: impervious fraction, vegetation fraction, drainage setback, on-plot retention) and immutable (nobody can: elevation, slope, HAND, river distance). We search for the smallest mutable change that moves a cell into a lower class: “High → Moderate if paved cover stays under ~42% and a 3 m drainage setback is kept.”

    Why new: the explainable-ML papers stop at SHAP attribution. Freezing the immutable features means the advice is always physically achievable — generic counterfactual methods do not guarantee that.

  6. N6 · Positive-unlabeled label model

    What: flooded cells are positive; everything else is unlabeled, not negative. We use PU-bagging with an Elkan–Noto class-prior correction, then train the LightGBM/XGBoost ensemble.

    Why it matters: all four treat sampled non-flood points as true negatives. But radar misses flooding under tree canopy and in urban radar shadow, and satellites revisit only every few days — a place may simply never have been imaged while under water. Calling those cells “safe” teaches the model something false.

Honesty line to keep in your pocket

“We ran a gap audit on our own earlier claims. Some did not survive — a hand-weighted score, five-class land cover, SHAP explanations and multi-year change overlays all already exist. We dropped or demoted those. What survived is N1–N6: the development-aware prediction, the flow-routed upslope feature, and validating against real development — with honest evaluation and honest labels underneath.”

Section 7

Architecture — what each stage actually does

Stage 1 · Data acquisition & harmonisation

Pull every layer for the Kochi / Periyar study area, reproject to UTM Zone 43N, resample everything onto one shared 10 m grid so layers line up cell-for-cell. Coarser layers (30 m DEM, ~5 km rainfall) are described honestly as context around a cell, not 10 m measurements. Five epochs: 2017 (pre-flood baseline), 2018 / 2019 / 2021 (flood events), 2024 (current). DEM = FABDEM v1.2 (canopy + buildings removed).

Stage 2A · Multi-epoch flood inventory (from Sentinel-1)

For each flood event: take Sentinel-1 SAR from the flood window, run the standard chain (radiometric calibration → speckle filter → terrain correction), convert to decibels, threshold with Edge-Otsu to get a water mask. Subtract the JRC permanent-water layer so rivers and backwaters are not counted as flooding. Three independent inventories (2018/2019/2021). DFO, Copernicus EMS / UNOSAT and local records corroborate.

Stage 2B · Built-up change detection (from Sentinel-2)

Segment land cover per epoch into five classes (building, vegetation, water, impervious/road, bare land) with DeepLabV3+ or U-Net, ImageNet-pretrained, fine-tuned on Kochi tiles. ESA WorldCover and Google Dynamic World give weak labels to bootstrap; OSM/Microsoft/Google footprints give building labels. Differencing epochs gives each cell a build year — which produces the treatment/control groups for the natural experiment and drives ΔUICA. Work is at grid-cell/cluster level, not individual buildings — a house is often smaller than one 10 m pixel.

Stage 3 · Flow-coupled feature engineering

From FABDEM: fill sinks, compute D8/D-infinity flow direction and accumulation, then elevation, slope, aspect, curvature, TWI, HAND. From vectors: distance to river, drainage line, road. From CHIRPS: rainfall frequency (days > 10 mm, 2010–2024). From Stage 2B: local built-up and vegetation fraction. Then the new features: UICA and ΔUICA.

Stage 4 · PU learning with leak-free validation

Positive = flooded cells; unlabeled = everything else. PU-bagging + Elkan–Noto prior correction → LightGBM + XGBoost ensemble. Evaluate three ways (random / spatial block CV / temporal hold-out). Every predicted cell carries a SHAP attribution, a conformal prediction interval, and an Area-of-Applicability flag that greys out cells whose features fall outside what the model learned from.

Stage 5 · Prospective dual prediction (the research core)

Produce S_current and S_developed for every cell; report Δ per parcel, per ward, and as a cumulative build-out scenario. Validate by the natural experiment (Section 8 below).

Stage 6 · Counterfactual recourse & dual dashboard

Planner view: ward-level susceptibility with the Area-of-Applicability mask, a ΔUICA hotspot map, the build-out simulator, and a ranked list of parcels where new construction would hurt neighbours most. Citizen view: search any location including empty land → current class, the uplift Δ if built on, the main factors in plain words, the confidence interval, and the top three actions that lower the class.

Tools & compute

Google Earth Engine (data prep, SAR, compositing — free tier, minutes) · WhiteboxTools / pysheds (flow routing, UICA, HAND, TWI — CPU) · PyTorch + segmentation-models-pytorch (Stage 2B — one free Colab/Kaggle GPU) · LightGBM / XGBoost / scikit-learn (CPU) · MAPIE (conformal) · SHAP + DiCE (explanation) · Streamlit/Flask + Leaflet/Folium (dashboard). Total data < 5 GB.

Section 9

Validation & why our numbers may look lower

How we check the model is right

We overlay our susceptibility map on the same grid as the combined flood-evidence layer (Sentinel-1 inventory + DFO + Copernicus EMS + local records). For each cell, “did this location fall inside documented flood extent” is a yes/no label. We compare against our score with:

  • PR-AUC — our primary metric, because flooded cells are a minority class and PR-AUC is more informative than ROC-AUC under that imbalance.
  • ROC-AUC — threshold-free separability, reported for comparability with the literature.
  • Risk Validation Rate — the direct structural equivalent of the base paper’s 84% Ground-Truth Index: of the cells we flag High / Very-High, what fraction fall inside documented flood extent.
  • Brier score (calibration) and conformal interval coverage (does the stated uncertainty hold up).

Section 10

The natural experiment — expect the hardest question here

The idea in one breath

We can test the development-aware claim against reality because the development already happened. Kerala’s recent history is the experiment: plots that were empty before the 2018 flood and have since been built on.

The setup

  • Treatment cells: unbuilt before the 2018 event, built by 2021 (identified by Stage 2B build-year detection).
  • Control cells: stayed unbuilt, matched to treatment cells on elevation, slope, HAND and distance to road (propensity matching).
  • The test: fit the model on pre-2018 data only. For the treatment cells, does S_developed predict their post-construction 2021 flood outcome better than S_current does? Report the ROC-AUC / PR-AUC difference and the % of cells that shift vulnerability class.

Answer ready before they ask it

“This is a quasi-experimental design, not a randomised one. People choose to build on flatter, more accessible land, so treatment and control cells differ systematically. Propensity matching reduces that but does not remove it. We will describe results as association under matching, never as a proven causal effect — and we say so in the report and here, rather than waiting to be caught on it.”

If the treatment group turns out too small

Widen the window to 2016–2024, widen the study area to the full Periyar basin, and report a power analysis rather than a weak result. This risk is in our plan with that mitigation already written down.

Section 11

Question bank — likely, tricky, and confusing

Concept & framing

Explain your project to someone who knows nothing about it.

“Flood maps today are built from where flooding has already happened, on land as it looks now. If you build a house on a green plot, that plot stops soaking up rain and starts shedding it onto the plots downhill — but the map never updated. We add features and a second prediction so the map can say what a plot becomes once it is built on, and we test that against plots in Kochi that were actually built on after 2018.”

Is this flood prediction or flood susceptibility? They are different.

“Susceptibility. We produce a standing map of relative tendency to flood, based on terrain and land cover. We do not forecast a specific storm or attach a probability to a future event. That is deliberate — it sidesteps the rainfall-timing error the base paper flags.”

Your title says “multi-temporal” but your base paper is single-snapshot. Contradiction?

“No — that is one of the things we change. The base paper predicts per image date. We build the full data stack for five years (2017–2024) so we can detect build years, compute ΔUICA, and run a temporal hold-out. Multi-temporal is our extension of their single-snapshot design, and it is exactly what their future-work section recommends.”

How is your “spatial-temporal analysis” different from the base paper’s — they claim the same phrase?

“Theirs is spatial-temporal in the sense of matching each SAR image to same-day rainfall. Ours is spatial-temporal in a different sense: multi-year epochs to measure change (ΔUICA) and to test whether a model fitted on 2018 still works in 2021. Same phrase, different mechanism.”

What is the single most novel thing here?

“Predicting two susceptibility values per plot — as it is, and as it would be once built — and validating the difference against development that really happened. UICA is the feature that makes the second value meaningful.”

Method

Define UICA. Give me the formula.

“Upslope Impervious Contributing Area. Take flow accumulation — follow every cell downhill and count what drains through each point — but weight each upstream cell by how paved it is. Formula: UICA(c) = sum over all cells u draining into c of impervious_fraction(u) × cell_area. It is one weighted flow-accumulation pass over the filled DEM. ΔUICA is its change between two years.”

How do you get flood labels without a hydraulic model?

“Two ways. We build a flood inventory directly from Sentinel-1 radar using the standard SAR → decibel → Edge-Otsu water-detection chain for the 2018, 2019 and 2021 events, minus the JRC permanent-water layer, corroborated by DFO and Copernicus EMS. And we train under positive-unlabeled learning, so we only need confirmed flooded locations — we never have to label anywhere ‘safe’.”

Positive-unlabeled learning — explain simply.

“We know where it flooded. We do not know that everywhere else is safe — radar misses water under trees and between buildings, and satellites pass only every few days, so a place might just never have been photographed while flooded. So we treat flooded cells as positive and everything else as ‘unlabeled’, and use a method that estimates the true positive rate and corrects for it, instead of calling unlabeled cells negative.”

How can you predict post-development susceptibility for land that hasn’t been developed?

“We query the trained model at a built-state feature vector: impervious fraction raised to the observed Kochi residential norm, vegetation reduced to match, and the UICA of every downstream cell updated for the new sealed surface. It is a counterfactual evaluation of the model, and we check it is right using the natural experiment on plots built between 2018 and 2021.”

Why LightGBM / XGBoost and not deep learning like your base paper?

“Our problem is tabular — for each cell we have ~15 engineered hydrological features and a yes/no flood label. Gradient boosting is the strongest, fastest tool for that, it runs on a laptop CPU, and it works cleanly with SHAP and with positive-unlabeled bagging. The closest Kerala study found LightGBM beat expert weighting 0.948 to 0.763. Deep learning still appears in our pipeline — but for the land-cover segmentation step, not the susceptibility model.”

What is HAND, and why does it matter that your papers don’t use it?

“Height Above Nearest Drainage — how far above the stream it drains to a cell sits, vertically. It is one of the strongest single flood predictors known, and none of our four reference papers includes it. Adding it is a low-cost, well-justified improvement.”

Why FABDEM instead of SRTM or plain Copernicus DEM?

“SRTM and raw Copernicus DEM include tree canopy and building tops in the ‘ground’ height. In a green, densely built district like Kochi, a coconut canopy over low land makes that land look higher than it is and puts it in the wrong flood class. FABDEM is the same Copernicus DEM with canopy and buildings machine-learned out. The Kerala paper names SRTM canopy interference as a limitation — FABDEM removes it.”

Spatial block cross-validation — why, in one line?

“Neighbouring raster cells are almost identical, so a random split effectively puts test data into the training set and inflates the score. We make each fold a whole sub-watershed so training and testing areas never touch.”

Data

Kerala is cloudy in the monsoon — how do you get optical imagery?

“Three ways: Sentinel-1 radar sees through cloud and is our flood-inventory source anyway; for land cover we use dry-season (December–February) median composites; and Google Dynamic World / ESA WorldCover provide ready-made land cover as a fallback. Cloud cover is a known constraint we designed around, not an afterthought.”

10 m pixels — can you really assess a single building?

“No, and we do not claim to. A single house is often smaller than one Sentinel-2 pixel. We score at grid-cell / building-cluster level and say so explicitly in every output. That honesty is a deliberate methodology point.”

Are Microsoft/Google building footprints reliable?

“They are AI-detected, not surveyed, so they have known noise in dense or informal settlements. We cross-check across three independent sources — Microsoft, Google, OSM — and manually spot-check a sample against the raw imagery before trusting them as ground truth.”

What if a data source goes down or rate-limits you?

“Every module has a documented fallback and logs when it uses one. For heavy needs like building footprints we already plan to move from live API calls to one-time bulk downloads, and we cache everything locally so re-running does not re-fetch.”

Novelty pressure

Isn’t AHP + LightGBM for Kerala already done — Kendagannaswamy 2025?

“Yes, and we reproduce both their models as baselines. That is the point of comparison, not our contribution. Our contribution is what they list as their own future work: temporal dynamics, cross-validation, a higher-resolution canopy-corrected DEM, and more advanced ML — plus development-aware prediction and UICA, which they do not touch at all.”

Explainable ML for floods already exists. So does change detection. What’s left?

“Both true — and we dropped those as standalone claims after auditing them. What is left and genuinely not in our reference set: the flow-routed upslope feature (UICA), the two-value prospective prediction with the uplift Δ, and validating a susceptibility model against observed development. Explainability and change detection are supporting tools here, not the headline.”

Isn’t this just applying an existing method to a new area?

“A new area and a new dashboard would not be a research contribution and we would not present them as one. The contribution is a question none of the four papers answers — does a plot’s flood susceptibility change once it is built on, by how much, and can that be predicted beforehand, including the runoff pushed onto neighbours downhill.”

Your expected AUC is lower than published work. Why is that acceptable?

“Because the published numbers are inflated by leaky validation. We report the leaky number and the leak-free number side by side; the gap between them is a result. A model that holds up on ground it has never seen is the one a planner should use.”

Scope, feasibility, use

Who actually uses this, and is it a certified flood model?

“Two users: a ward planner watching where growth is loading risk, and a citizen checking a plot before building. It is an advisory decision-support indicator — relative vulnerability with a stated confidence — not a statutory hydraulic model, and we label it that way everywhere.”

Can you finish this in one semester?

“The core deliverable — flood inventory, change detection, flow-coupled features, PU model, leak-free validation, and experiments E1 and E2 — lands by week 12. The full dual prediction and its natural-experiment test is the top extension for weeks 13–14. We sequenced it so a complete, defensible result exists even if the last stage runs long. E1 alone is a publishable finding about validation honesty for Kerala.”

Where does rainfall enter if you don’t use live storms?

“As a long-run climate statistic: the number of days per year above 10 mm from CHIRPS over 2010–2024. It tells the model how wet a place’s climate is without tying any prediction to a specific storm date.”

What does success look like — give a number.

“A defensible PR-AUC / ROC-AUC under spatial block CV — we would be satisfied around 0.82–0.88 — plus a measurable, significant PR-AUC gain from adding UICA (E2), and a measurable, honestly-reported under-estimation from the naive current-state lookup (E3). Beating 0.948 is not the goal.”

What is your explainability, concretely?

“Every scored cell gets a SHAP breakdown — which factors pushed the score up or down — and a constrained counterfactual: the smallest change in things a builder can control (paved fraction, setback, on-plot retention) that moves the cell to a lower class, with elevation and slope frozen so the advice is always physically achievable.”

What is your single biggest risk?

“Time, not data. GIS and CV debugging — alignment, resampling, model convergence — always takes longer than estimated. We built slack in and prioritised a working end-to-end pipeline early over perfecting any one stage. The one true data risk is Sentinel-1 coverage at the 2018 flood peak, which we check in Week 1 with a written fallback.”

If you had to cut scope tomorrow, what goes?

“The citizen dashboard and the conformal-uncertainty layer go first — they are extensions 3 and 4. Coastal and tidal flooding and climate-change scenarios are already declared out of scope. The core model, the UICA experiment and the leak-free evaluation stay — those are the contribution.”

Section 12

Limitations — volunteer these before you’re asked

A panel trusts a team that names its own limits. Work one or two of these into the talk unprompted.

  • Resolution. Sentinel-2 is 10 m — smaller than many houses. We score at grid-cell / cluster level, never per building.
  • DEM. A 30 m DEM gives terrain context around a cluster, not exact per-building height.
  • The natural experiment is quasi-experimental. People choose where to build; matching narrows but does not close the gap. Results are “association under matching”, not proven causation.
  • Counterfactuals are what-if statements under our model, not engineering guarantees. “High → Moderate if paved cover stays under 42%” describes the model’s response, not a certified hydraulic outcome.
  • S_developed uses a typical built density, not a specific building plan. A given plot may be built more or less densely.
  • UICA is a runoff proxy, not a drainage model. It follows terrain flow paths and ignores stormwater pipes, culverts and pumping. In a serviced urban area the real routing can differ.
  • Rainfall is a long-run frequency, not an event. The cost of sidestepping the timing problem is that our map cannot speak to any specific storm.
  • Flood risk is multi-causal. River discharge, tides, soil permeability and drainage maintenance all matter and are outside our data. We model the terrain / land-cover side and say so.
  • DFO is coarse (~250 m) and not building-level ground truth — used as one of several evidence sources, not sole proof.
  • Change detection can misfire on clouds, shadows, seasonal vegetation, or construction materials — mitigated by manual sample verification, not treated as error-free.
  • Ethics. A public vulnerability map can be misused — to depress land prices or justify unequal services. Mitigations: publish the Area-of-Applicability mask, report confidence intervals not bare classes, use relative-vulnerability language, and state the output is advisory, not a statutory survey.

Section 15

If you remember only five things

  1. The one-liner. “We predict how flood-prone land will be after it is built on — not just how it looks today — and we test it against plots in Kochi that were built on after 2018.”
  2. The word is “susceptibility.” Not prediction, not forecast, not risk. A standing map of relative tendency to flood.
  3. Three things are genuinely new against our four papers: UICA (paved surface routed downhill), the dual prediction (S_current, S_developed, Δ), and natural-experiment validation. The rest is disciplined method — honest evaluation, honest labels, actionable advice.
  4. Lower AUC is a finding, not a failure. Published 0.94–0.99 values are inflated by leaky random splits. We report the leaky and leak-free numbers side by side.
  5. Name your limits first. Cluster-level not per-building; quasi-experiment not causal proof; advisory indicator not a certified flood model; terrain/land-cover side only. Saying it first is the strongest move you have.
